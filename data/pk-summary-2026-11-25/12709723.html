Pharmacokinetics of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine in pregnancy and lactation

Abstract

Background

The aim of this prospective clinical trial was to investigate the pharmacokinetics offluoxetine and its active metabolite, norfluoxetine, during pregnancy, delivery, and lactation in mothers and their infants.

Methods

Eleven mothers taking fluoxetine and their infants were enrolled in the study. A control group of 10 women who were not taking psychotropic medication were prospectively matched for confounding obstetric characteristics at the time of delivery. Trough plasma samples and breast milk samples were collected from mother-infant pairs during pregnancy, at delivery, and up to 2 months after delivery in the fluoxetine group. The pregnancy outcome was recorded, and the growth and neurologic development of the children were followed up to the age of 1 year in both study groups.

Results

The fluoxetine dose from 20 mg to 40 mg once daily resulted in relatively low trough fluoxetine-norfluoxetine concentrations during pregnancy (range, 317-850 nmol/L). The mean norfluoxetine/fluoxetine metabolic ratio was 2.4-fold higher during late pregnancy than at 2 months after delivery (P = .0072). At delivery, the infant plasma fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations were 65% and 72%, respectively, of those found in mothers. The mean estimated infant exposures from breast milk to fluoxetine-norfluoxetine were 2.4% and 3.8% of the maternal weight-adjusted daily dose at age 2 weeks and age 2 months, respectively. The pregnancy outcome, as well as the growth and neurologic development of all infants up to 1 year of age, was normal.

Conclusion

Common clinical doses of fluoxetine resulted in relatively low concentrations of fluoxetine during pregnancy, which can be explained at least partly by increased demethylation of fluoxetine by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6. This might indicate that these low blood levels could lead to therapeutic failure, and clinicians should be alert to this possibility so that depression in pregnancy is not undertreated.

Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics (2003) 73, 330–337; doi: 10.1016/S0009-9236(02)17634-X

The selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor fluoxetine is an extensively prescribed antidepressant throughout the world.1 Because mood and anxiety disorders are common in women of childbearing age, there is a clinical need for effective and safe pharmacotherapy of mental disorders during pregnancy and lactation. Although fluoxetine is extensively used during pregnancy and lactation, its pharmacokinetics and metabolism are poorly characterized in pregnant women and in newborns.

Fluoxetine is primarily metabolized in the liver by N-demethylation to norfluoxetine, which is further converted to conjugated metabolites and then excreted in the urine.2,3,4 Norfluoxetine has the same pharmacologic activity as the parent compound, and there is a good correlation between blood and brain fluoxetine-norfluoxetine concentrations.5,6 The polymorphic cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6 is the main enzyme participating in this oxidation, and the high interindividual variability in the activity of this enzyme influences the relative concentrations of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine in plasma (and the brain).7 Fluoxetine and norfluoxetine are highly lipid-soluble compounds and would thus be expected to cross the placenta, as well as to be excreted to breast milk. Furthermore, the long elimination half-lives of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine (1-3 days and 7-15 days, respectively) suggest the risk for accumulation into breast milk and especially into the fetus or infant.

Epidemiologic studies of hundreds of women exposed to off-label use of fluoxetine during pregnancy have indicatedthat fluoxetine does not increase the rate of major fetal malformations.8,9,10 Nulman et al11 reported that the neurocognitive development and language skills of children who were between the ages of 16 and 86 months and who had been exposed in utero for varying lengths of time to fluoxetine were found to be similar tothose of control subjects not exposed to psychotropic medication. However, 3 anecdotal case reports suggest that high perinatal concentrations of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine may be associated with adverse neurologic effects in newborns.12,13,14

Some case series on infants' exposure to fluoxetine during breast-feeding indicate that the total dose of fluoxetine-norfluoxetine to infants is usually quite low.15,16,17,18 However, these earlier reports indicate considerable interindividual differences in the breast milk and infant concentrations. Moreover, some adverse effects such as crying, irritability, poor feeding, and reduced growth have been described in breast-fed infants.17,19 However, practically nothing is known about the maternal concentrations of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine during pregnancy, transplacental transfer of fluoxetine, or the ability of the infant to metabolize fluoxetine and norfluoxetine.

The aim of this prospective clinical trial was to investigate the pharmacokinetics of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine during pregnancy, delivery, and lactation in mothers and their infants, as well as to characterize the effects of this drug exposure on the growth and neurologic development of fetuses and infants during the first year of life.

METHODS

Patients and ethics

Twenty-one pregnant women were recruited to this prospective study. Eleven of these women were taking fluoxetine during pregnancy and lactation. Fluoxetine was prescribed by psychiatrists for the treatment of depression (n = 5) or panic disorder (n = 6). The median dose of fluoxetine ingested by the women was 20 mg (range, 20–40 mg) daily. Six of the women already used fluoxetine before conception, and they used fluoxetine throughout pregnancy and lactation. Five women started taking fluoxetine later during pregnancy (ie, at 22, 27, 31, 32, and 35 weeks of gestation) and continued with fluoxetine after delivery. One of these women used clonazepam continuously before her medication was changed to fluoxetine at the thirty-first week of pregnancy. Four of the women with panic disorder occasionally took benzodiazepines (0.5- to 2-mg dose of alprazolam or 1- to 2-mg dose of lorazepam) during pregnancy).

A control group of 10 women (there was 1 dropout in the control group) with no psychotropic medication was prospectively matched for confounding obstetric characteristics (age, gravidity, parity, gestational weeks, and mode of delivery) at the time of delivery. One woman in each study group used levothyroxine for the treatment of hypothyroidism.

The study protocol was approved by the Joint Commission on Ethics of Turku University and Turku University Central Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all mothers before enrollment in the study.

Study design and assessments

Pregnancy

The women taking fluoxetine visited the maternity unit of Turku University Hospital 3 times during pregnancy (ie, at 20–24, 28–32, and 36–37 weeks of gestation). The physical and ultrasonographic (including Doppler survey) examinations were performed at each of the 3 visits, and venous blood samples (5 mL) were taken at the last visit for the analysis of plasma concentrations of fluoxetine and its main metabolite, norfluoxetine. Blood samples were drawn just before the mothers took their daily fluoxetine dose (trough concentration). The blood samples were centrifuged, and plasma was kept at −20°C until analysis.

Delivery and early puerperium

At the time of delivery, before the umbilical cord was cut, blood samples (5 mL) were drawn from both the umbilical artery and the umbilical vein, and the birth weight, Apgar score, umbilical artery pH, and body temperature were routinely recorded. At the same time, a venous blood sample was taken from the mother. On postpartum days 2 and 4, venous blood samples were drawn from the mother and the infant (1-2 mL) just before the mother took her daily dose of fluoxetine. The infant's venous blood sample was taken with a needle from the forearm or head. On the morning of postpartum day 4, a breast milk sample (15 mL) was collected by manual emptying or by breast pump. The breast milk samples were collected immediately before a feeding and before the mothers took their daily dose of fluoxetine (ie, at the same time as the blood samples). The milk samples were stored at −70°C until analysis.

Follow-up after delivery

Venous blood samples from the mother and infant were taken and a breast milk sample was again collected at 2 weeks and at 2 months after delivery with the same methods described previously. The blood samples were collected only from nursing mothers and breast-fed infants. The length of full breast-feeding and the daily volume of infant formula during partial feeding were recorded. The growth and development of the infants were followed until age 1 year in the Neonatal and Child Intensive Care Unit of Turku University Hospital. The developmental assessment included a careful neurologic examination by a pediatrician and a physiotherapist, at the corrected ages of 2 weeks and 2, 6, and 12 months. In the assessment of neurologic development, modified Gesell developmental schedules including gross and fine motor functions, tonus, speech development, sensory screening, and social behavior were used.20 The outcome was classified into 2 categories—normal or abnormal.

Bioanalytic methods

The plasma and breast milk concentrations of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine were analyzed by reversed-phase HPLC with ultraviolet detection according to Kelly et al.21 The interassay coefficients of variation for both plasma and milk samples were 2.2% and 3.4% for fluoxetine and norfluoxetine at mean concentrations of 120 nmol/L and 125 nmol/L, respectively. The lower limit of quantitation was 10 nmol/L for both plasma and milk samples for both analytes.

The fat content of milk samples was determined by the international standard gravimetric method.22 The fat content is expressed as a percentage by mass. The reproducibility of the method was 0.04%. The lower limit of quantification was 0.05%.

Data analysis

The mean (±SD) maternal and infant plasma and breast milk concentrations were calculated at each collection time. In addition to actual concentrations, the concentration data of both fluoxetine and norfluoxetine are also given as dose-corrected values to correspond with the defined daily dose (DDD) of 20 mg of fluoxetine. The norfluoxetine/fluoxetine metabolic ratios were calculated from the trough concentrations measured from the blood samples taken at the end of pregnancy (ie, at 36–37 gestational weeks) and 2 months after delivery to obtain a measure of maternal CYP2D6 activity. The mean (±SD) child/mother plasma concentration ratios and milk/plasma (M/P) ratios were calculated at each collection time. If the infant plasma concentration was under the quantitation limit (ie, 10 nmol/L), the value 5 nmol/L was used in calculations. The elimination half-life of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine was estimated by least-squares regression analysis of the linear part of the log concentration–time curve to describe the capacity of the infants to metabolize these compounds. It should be emphasized that these values are rough estimates of the elimination rate for fluoxetine, because only 2 time points could be used to calculate the half-life in all but 2 infants.

The exposure of infants to fluoxetine through breast milk was calculated by the method described by Atkinson et al23: Infant dose (expressed as a percentage of maternaldaily dose) = [Cmilk × Vmilk × 100]/Dmaternal, where Cmilk is the steady-state concentration in the breast milk, Vmilk is the volume of breast milk ingested, and Dmaternal is the maternal daily drug dose per kilogram. An average infant milk intake of 150 mL/kg per day23 and 100% oral bioavailability of fluoxetine were assumed.

Statistical analyses

The unpaired Student t test (2-tailed) or Mann-Whitney U test (for nonnormal and categoric data) was used for statistical comparison of demographics and outcome between the fluoxetine and control groups. The Student paired t test (2-tailed) was used for comparison of metabolic ratios during pregnancy and lactation. A repeated-measures ANOVA was used in the comparison of the drug concentrations, child/mother ratios, and M/P ratios at the different time points. Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate the association between the fat content of breast milk and fluoxetine and norfluoxetine M/P values. The 2-tailed level of statistical significance was set at P < .05. All of the data were analyzed with the SAS statistical program, version 8 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

Maternal plasma concentrations

The trough fluoxetine concentrations detected from the blood samples taken at 36 to 37 weeks of gestation (n = 8) were low (ranging from 46 nmol/L to 351 nmol/L) (Table II) compared with concentrations based on samples from depressive patients in the nonpregnant state treated with daily fluoxetine doses from 20 mg to 40 mg (259 nmol/L to 647 nmol/L).24 Similarly, the values for the active moiety (ie, the sum of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine) were low (ranging from 317 nmol/L to 850 nmol/L compared with concentrations from 700 nmol/L to 1363 nmol/L in depressive patients in the nonpregnant state24). Although the active moiety concentrations were low during pregnancy, a dose increment was made in only 1 patient (from 20 mg to 40 mg at 36 weeks of gestation). The trough plasma concentrations were 98 nmol/L and 370 nmol/L for fluoxetine and fluoxetine-norfluoxetine, respectively, before the dose increment.

The maternal trough fluoxetine concentrations were still low during the first days after delivery (n = 11) but increased significantly thereafter; at 2 weeks (n = 9) and at 2 months (n = 8) after delivery, the maternal fluoxetine concentrations were 55% (P = .036) and 61% (P = .0028) higher than those during late pregnancy, respectively. However, the maternal norfluoxetine concentrations remained rather stable during pregnancy and the puerperium (Table II), and thus fluoxetine-norfluoxetine concentrations were only 32% higher 2 months after delivery than during late pregnancy (P = .018). The mean (±SD) norfluoxetine/fluoxetine metabolic ratio was 2.4-fold higher during pregnancy (3.3 ± 1.4) than at 2 months after delivery (1.4 ± 0.8, P = .0072), suggesting increased fluoxetine demethylation during pregnancy.

Infant plasma concentrations

There was a gradual but significant decline in the infant fluoxetine concentrations at 2 months after delivery (ANOVA, P = .0014), as well as in the active moiety concentration values (P < .0001) (Table II). At 2 weeks after delivery (n = 10), the trough fluoxetine concentration was already under the quantitation limit (10 nmol/L) in all but 2 infants, but norfluoxetine concentrations were detectable (ranging from 53 nmol/L to 312 nmol/L) in all infants. It should be noted that the fluoxetine (92 nmol/L and 107 nmol/L) and norfluoxetine (253 nmol/L and 312 nmol/L) concentrations were high in these 2 infants at 2 weeks. At 2 months (n = 8), all infant fluoxetine concentrations were under the detection limit, but low norfluoxetine concentrations were still detected (ranging from 12 to 48 nmol/L) in 6 infants. The mean estimated infant elimination half-lives for fluoxetine and norfluoxetine were 5 days (range, 2-11 days) and 14 days (range, 8-28 days), respectively.

The infant plasma fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations (samples taken from umbilical vein, n = 8) were 65% and 72% of those found in mothers at the time of delivery, respectively (Table II, Fig 1). For both fluoxetine and norfluoxetine, the concentrations in the umbilical artery were 62% of the corresponding maternal concentrations and thus were similar to those found in the umbilical vein. The child/mother plasma concentration ratio of fluoxetine had declined quite rapidly and significantly; on postpartum day 4, the mean ratio was already almost 50% lower than at delivery (P = .036). However, the decrease in the norfluoxetine child/mother ratio was markedly slower.

Milk concentrations

The mean fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations in the breast milk were relatively low (Table II). This resulted in mean estimated infant exposure to fluoxetine-norfluoxetine of 2.4% and 3.8% of the maternal weight-adjusted daily dose at ages 2 weeks (n = 9) and 2 months (n = 8), respectively.

The M/P values ranged from 0.3 to 2.2 for fluoxetine and from 0.1 to 1.7 for norfluoxetine (Fig 2). Although the fluoxetine and norfluoxetine M/P ratios declined by about 35% (P = .095; power, 58%) and 55% (P = .07; power, 79%), respectively, from day 4 to day 14, these differences only reached a statistical trend. The milk fat content ranged from 1.0% to 6.7%, but there was no correlation between milk fat content and fluoxetine (rank correlation coefficient [rs] = 0.067; P = .75) or norfluoxetine (rs = −0.063; P = .76) M/P values.

Clinical follow-up data

The development (morphologic characteristics and growth) of fetuses exposed to fluoxetine was normal at ultrasonographic examinations, with the exception of 1 mild case of polyhydramnios seen at 37 weeks. The etiology of the case of polyhydramnios remained unclear, but the infant was born normal and healthy. The maternal uterine, umbilical, and fetal middle cerebral artery Doppler velocity waveforms (expressed as pulsatility index) were all within the normal range (data not shown).

The pregnancy outcomes were similar in the fluoxetine group and the control group, with the exception of the mean 15-minute Apgar score, which was lower in the fluoxetine group (Table III). No malformations were detected, and there was no difference in infant birth weights between groups.

The weight gain in infants was similar in the fluoxetine and control groups, and all infants had body weights within the normal range at 12 months (Table III). The neurologic development of all 21 infants was normal at age 1 year.

DISCUSSION

This prospective clinical trial indicated that a 20- to 40-mg daily dose of fluoxetine during pregnancy results in lower trough plasma concentrations of fluoxetine and fluoxetine plus norfluoxetine than those detected in a clinical setting with similar doses in the nonpregnant state.24 These low concentrations are consistent with the physiologic changes during pregnancy resulting in increased hepatic blood flow, as well as increased volume of distribution and decreased binding to plasma proteins of many drugs. However, we found 58% higher norfluoxetine/fluoxetine metabolic ratios during pregnancy compared with those at 2 months after delivery, suggesting markedly increased fluoxetine demethylation by CYP2D6 during pregnancy. Similarly, 2 previous studies have demonstrated that pregnancy increases CYP2D6 activity while having little effect on the activities of CYP2C19 or 3A4.25,26

The umbilical vein fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations were high at birth (ie, 65% and 72% of the maternal concentrations, respectively), indicating the limited protective role of the placenta at steady state. However, the pregnancy outcomes were similar in the fluoxetine group and the control group, with the exception of the somewhat lower mean 15-minute Apgar score in the fluoxetine-exposed infants. At 2 days post partum, the infant fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations were still high; the DDD-corrected active moiety ranged from 151 nmol/L to 573 nmol/L. Previously, only 2 case reports have provided pharmacokinetic data on both fluoxetine and norfluoxetine during the early perinatal period.12,13 We calculated DDD-corrected active moieties from these reports and obtained concentrations of 267 nmol/L and 395 nmol/L, respectively, which are consistent with our own results. In our study the high infant fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations found during the early postnatal period declined gradually during the first 2 months of life.

Previous studies have suggested that CYP2D6 enzyme surges within hours after birth although proteins could not have been detected in fetal samples, and, moreover, the catalytic activity of this enzyme gradually increases during the first postnatal month.27,28,29,30 Accordingly, our finding that there was no significant difference in the fluoxetine and norfluoxetine child/mother ratios calculated either from the umbilical artery or from vein drug concentrations supports the negligible catalytic activity of CYP2D6 in human fetal liver. After delivery, the infant norfluoxetine concentrations declined slowly, as indicated by the long infant elimination half-life (ie, from 8 to 28 days). This finding is in accordance with the slow development of infant glucuronidation capacity compared with oxidation capacity during the first months of life.31 In addition, there seems to be extensive interindividual variation in this glucuronidation capacity during the early neonatal period, ranging from 8% to 100% of adult values.32 The slowly and individually developing glucuronidation capacity, together with the genetic polymorphism of CYP2D6 enzyme activity, might explain the high fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations measured in 2 infants at age 2 weeks, because their mothers' plasma and breast milk concentrations were similar to other mothers' concentrations.

As indicated by M/P ratios of about 0.6 and 0.3, fluoxetine and norfluoxetine were both excreted to the breast milk but not accumulated in the milk. This finding is somewhat unexpected considering the physicochemical properties of these compounds (ie, high lipid solubility and high values of the negative logarithm of the acid ionization constant [pKa]), which should favor accumulation in the breast milk. Furthermore, we did not find any correlation between fluoxetine and norfluoxetine M/P concentration ratio and breast milk fat content, which might be expected on the basis of the high lipid solubility of these compounds.33 However, fluoxetine and norfluoxetine are highly bound to plasma proteins (>95%), which might restrict the distribution of these compounds to the breast milk.

Recently, Kristensen et al17 reported M/P values of 0.68 for fluoxetine and 0.56 for norfluoxetine in 14 nursing mothers and mean infant (mean age, 3.4 months) exposure for fluoxetine plus norfluoxetine of 6.8% of the weight-adjusted maternal daily dose. Similarly, Taddio et al15 reported corresponding infant (median age, 185 days) exposure of 6.5%,34 and Yoshida et al16 reported exposure from 3% to 10% (infant age range, 27–280 days). In our study the mean estimated infant exposures (fluoxetine plus norfluoxetine) were somewhat lower, 2.4% and 3.8% of the maternal weight-adjusted daily dose at ages 2 weeks and 2 months, respectively. This is likely a result of earlier time points of measurement; the older the infants are, the less the difference between infant's and mother's weight and the higher the estimated weight-adjusted dose. It should be noted that a limitation of our study was the collection of a single breast milk and plasma sample instead of multiple samples at intervals throughout the day.35 However, for drugs such as fluoxetine with a high volume of distribution and a long half-life of elimination, calculation of M/P values from the single milk and plasma concentration data seems to give a fair estimate of drug excretion into milk.17,26,36 Nevertheless, our results support the current understanding that the exposure of the nursed child to fluoxetine plus norfluoxetine is low, making breast-feeding possible. Normal weight gain and neurologic development of the fluoxetine-exposed infants also support the safety of breast-feeding.

In conclusion, fluoxetine doses from 20 to 40 mg resulted in low fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations during pregnancy. This might indicate that these low blood levels during pregnancy could lead to therapeutic failure, and clinicians should be alert to this possibility so that depression in pregnancy is not undertreated. However, the infant fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations were high at delivery and during the early postnatal period, indicating relatively high transplacental transfer and slow elimination of these compounds by the infant. Fluoxetine and norfluoxetine concentrations in breast milk were relatively low, and the exposure of infants via breast milk was low.
