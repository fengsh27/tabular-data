Abstract

Objective

To determine nifedipine plasma concentrations after a loading dose of nifedipine 10 mg capsules, 40 mg over 1 h followed by slow-release tablets (60 mg/d) versus gastrointestinal therapeutic system (GITS) tablets (90 mg/d) for tocolysis.

Study design

Prospective study in 14 pregnant women treated for threatened preterm labor.

Results

Following capsule administration there was a rapid rise in plasma concentration of drug achieving a peak of 97.5 μg/l (median) at 1 h, then declined to 59.5 μg/l (median) at 5 h. The concentration measured at 7200 min (120 h) was non-significantly higher in the slow-release group (median 25.5, range 6.9–67.2 μg/l) than in the GITS group (median 14.6, range 6.0–20.0 μg/l). Area under the curve (AUC) increased with the applied dose in both groups in a linear regression. Headache was more frequent in the slow-release group than in the GITS group (P = 0.001).

Conclusions

GITS tablets 90 mg/d are an alternative dosage regimen to previous used slow-release tablets 60 mg/d for tocolysis with similar pharmacokinetic profile and a good tolerance. However, tocolysis with GITS tablets is simpler than that with slow-release tablets and may be associated with a higher compliance. GITS tablets are therefore also qualified for home monitoring.

1. Introduction

Nifedipine, a calcium channel blocker, is a smooth muscle relaxant used mainly as a vasodilator to treat angina and hypertension. Smooth muscle relaxing effect will be achieved by blocking the influx of extracellular calcium into the myometrial cell. The first report of the tocolytic efficacy dates from 1980 [1]. It was then investigated in randomised prospective trials totaling over 1000 patients, using a variety of dosage regimens based on slow-release tablets [2], [3]. The regimen of Papatsonis et al. presented a loading dose of nifedipine 10 mg capsules, ≤40 mg over 1 h followed by a maintenance dose of 60–160 mg of slow-release tablets per day [3]. Thereby nifedipine revealed several important advantages over intravenous ritodrine: longer postponement of delivery, fewer maternal side effects, and fewer neonatal intensive care admissions, not to mention oral dosing and lower drug and administration costs. On this basis, although nifedipine is not licensed for tocolysis in Switzerland, we trialled it in the Department of Obstetrics at Zurich University Hospital in a formulation providing superior slow-release characteristics – nifedipine gastrointestinal therapeutic system, GITS (Adalat CR®, Bayer Switzerland) – at a maintenance dose of 30–150 (mean 90) mg/d titrated to clinical response.
In cardiovascular patients, therapeutic blood levels are 10–100 μg/l (29–289 nmol/l; 1 μg/l = 2.9 nmol/l) [4]. In a pharmacokinetic study with healthy non-pregnant volunteers the steady state concentration after nifedipine prolonged action (slow-release) tablets given every 12 h in two doses was equal to that after one tablet nifedipine GITS 60 mg and did not exceed the limit of 100 μg/l [5]. These data demonstrate clearly the lower absorption of nifedipine from GITS than from slow-release tablets. However, first data from a non-randomised observational study in our patients undergoing tocolysis with nifedipine GITS tablets (max 150 mg/d) showed a wide variability in nifedipine concentrations above this range [6]. To verify pharmacokinetic data in pregnancy we conducted therefore a prospective randomised study on the pharmcokinetic profile of nifedipine in GITS and slow-release tablets in patients undergoing tocolysis.

2. Methods

2.1. Patients

Following approval from the institutional review board of the Department of Obstetric at Zurich University Hospital, 14 pregnant women with preterm labor (seven per group) admitted to our hospital were consecutively recruited and randomly assigned to one of the treatment group with their informed consent. Inclusion criteria were singleton pregnancy, gestational age >19.0 and ≤35.0 weeks and preterm labor. Gestational age was calculated from the first day of the last menstrual period and first trimester ultrasound. Preterm labor was defined as painful CTG-recorded contractions >2/h and/or cervical effacement detected by vaginal examination or vaginal ultrasound scanning (cervical length < 2.5 cm) with or without rupture of membranes or vaginal bleeding. Exclusion criteria were cervical dilation >3 cm, fever (>37.5 °C), urinary tract infection (positive nitrite and microbiological culture), preeclampsia, abruptio, placental abnormality, acute serious maternal disease, co-treatment with drugs known to interact with the pharmacokinetics of nifedipine via cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), the ingestion of alcohol and grapefruit (fruit and juice) 1 week before and the participation in a clinical trial in the preceding month.

2.2. Protocol

According to the prospective open randomised design, treatments were allocated by our hospital pharmacy in a computer-generated random sequence using sealed opaque envelopes allocating patients to the treatment group, either nifedipine slow-release (group 1) or nifedipine GITS tablets (group 2). A loading dose of nifedipine 40 mg over 1 h (four capsules 10 mg every 15 min; Nifedipin Mepha®, Switzerland) was administered to all patients followed by a maintenance dose with nifedipine slow-release or GITS tablets respectively. Slow-release tablets 20 mg (Adalat retard® 20 mg, Bayer Switzerland) were given all 8 h for a total dose of 60 mg/24 h in group 1 and nifedipine GITS tablets 30 and 60 mg, respectively (Adalat CR® 30 mg, 60 mg, Bayer Switzerland) were given every 12 h for a total dose of 90 mg/24 h in group 2, for at least 120 h. These regimens are based (1) on the fact that the dosage of slow-release tablets 60 mg per day is well described for tocolysis [3], (2) on the hypothesis that absorption of nifedipine from slow-release is higher than from GITS tablets resulting in a lower dose which is necessary to produce the same effect (plasma level and/or tocolytic effect), and (3) on our experience that a mean daily dose of GITS tablets titrated to clinical response corresponds to 90 mg.
Blood samples were obtained following an exact time schedule with seven samples on study day 1 and four samples each at 24 h-intervals between days 2 and 5. Supine blood pressure, heart rate measurements and side effects according to a fixed protocol were recorded throughout the study, always immediately before the next blood sampling; co-medication was also recorded daily. Concomitant food during the first hour of the study and within at least 2 h after ingestion of a nifedipine tablet as well as the ingestion of alcohol and grapefruit (fruit and juice) during the study were not allowed. The end of the study protocol was at the end of day 5; however, tocolysis was continued as long as clinically necessary.

2.3. Nifedipine assay

Venous blood samples were taken in EDTA tubes wrapped with aluminium foil to protect it from light exposure; plasma was frozen at −20 °C until analysis.
Quantification of nifedipine in plasma samples was performed by liquid chromatography–turbo ion spray–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–TIS–tandem MS/MS). In order to minimize photodegradation of nifedipine (which may occur with a half-life as short as 2–3 min), complete sample preparation was done in a dark-room. Nitrendipine as internal standard was added to 100 μl of plasma samples. Separation of analyte from matrix components and potentially interfering compounds was carried out by isocratic reverse phase HPLC on a Merck Chromolith C18 (50 mm × 4.6 mm) column with acetonitrile/5 mM acetic acid pH 3.9 (6 + 4) at a flow rate of 1 ml/min using an Agilent 1100C binary pump and CTC-Pal autosampler. Selective ion fragments were detected by the API 4000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems/Sciex) in the multiple-reaction monitoring mode. The turbo ion spray temperature was 600 °C, and the ionization voltage was set to 3500 V. The precursor and product ions were at m/z 345.0 and 121.9 for nifedipine and 359.0 and 121.9 for nitrendipine, respectively. The detection limit was 0.2 μg/l, and the intraassay (n = 10) coefficient of variation was found to be 2.7%.

2.4. Data collection, objective and endpoint

Dosage, time of blood sampling, blood pressure and heart rate, co-medication and side effects were compiled from special protocols recorded daily by the study nurse; maternal and neonatal data were collected from medical records. Primary endpoint was the plasma concentration profile over the first 5 days. Differences in the nifedipine plasma levels of >20 μg/l (median) between the two groups were considered as clinically relevant.

2.5. Statistics

Data were expressed as mean ± S.D. or SE and/or as median (figures). The sample size of 14 was adequate (power >90%, α = 0.05) for detecting a difference >20 μg/l between the median plasma concentrations at 120 h in both groups within the range of <6 to >100 μg/l.

2.5.1. Pharmacokinetic analysis

Areas under the plasma concentration–time curves were calculated by the log-linear trapezoidal rule using the Stata program (Stata 8.0 for Windows, Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA). Cl was calculated as dose (total mg nifedipine in 120 h) per AUC0–120 h. The maximum nifedipine plasma concentration (Cmax) was read directly from the data corresponding to the concentration at 60 min along with the plasma concentration at steady state corresponding to the concentration measured at 120 h.

2.5.2. Statistical tests

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for normality. Means were tested for between-group differences using the Mann–Whitney U test, the unpaired two-tailed t-test of log values, respectively (continuous values) and the fishers exact test (numeric values). A P value <0.05 was used in all tests.

3. Results

3.1. Patients

Both groups did not differ in demographic or obstetric variables (Table 1). Tocolysis after study was continued until 36.0 weeks (median; range: 27.14–37.14) in both groups. Women gave birth at 38.0 weeks (median of all; range: 30.43–41.43). In one case (group 1) and three cases (group 2), respectively, neonates were preterm. There were no neonates with a birth percentile <5 or an Apgar at 1 min <7.

3.2. Nifedipine concentrations

Nifedipine plasma concentrations were log-normally distributed in both groups.
Nifedipine levels rose rapidly during the administration of 10 mg capsules and peaked after the first hour at 108.7 (median 97.5; N = 14) μg/l, then rapidly declined to 57.3 (median 59.5; N = 14) μg/l at 5 h, while the concentration in the group with slow-release tablets (mean 63.5 ± S.E. 13.6; median 64.9 μg/l; n = 7) was slightly higher than in patients with GITS tablets (mean 51.04 ± S.E. 9.32; median 58.2 μg/l; n = 7) (n.s.) (Fig. 1). Concentrations measured at 1 h varied within a wide range (19.9–292.0 μg/l; N = 14). The concentration measured at 7200 min (120 h) which was considered as steady-state concentration was non-significantly higher in the slow-release group (mean 32.0 ± S.E. 9.0; median 25.5; range 6.9–67.2 μg/l) than in the GITS group (mean 14.5 ± S.E. 1.8; median 14.6; range 6.0–20.0 μg/l) (Table 2). Both AUC and Cl as well as AUC normalised for dose per bodyweight and Cl normalised for bodyweight were similar in the two groups. AUC values increased proportionally with the applied dose in both groups (Fig. 2A and B). However, linear relationship between AUC and nifedipine dose was more closed in the GITS than in the slow-release group reason to the smaller inter-individual variation in dose related AUC's in patients with GITS.

3.3. Tolerance

Blood pressure as well as heart rate varied with time of application and nifedipine concentration. Most changes were found in the first hour of nifedipine therapy. However, no changes were clinically relevant (Table 3). All reported maternal adverse events were mild or moderate and transitory. None prompted treatment discontinuation. Headache was the most frequently reported event within the capsule loading dose (75%) and during the maintenance dosage where it was more frequent in the slow-release tablets group (37.5%) than in the GITS group (10.7%), P = 0.001. Other adverse effects were rare in both groups, such as dizziness or nausea (only one woman had nausea before and 30 min after the first nifedipine capsule).

4. Discussion

In meta-analyses calcium channel blockers were considered safer and more effective than betamimetics [2] and nifedipine was recommended as first line tocolytic agent [7]. Although Papatsonis et al. presented already in 1997 a dosage regimen with nifedipine slow-release tablets [3] nifedipine is not licensed in Europe for tocolysis and no study existed so far about nifedipine plasma concentrations after slow-release or GITS tablets in pregnant women.
In the present study nifedipine concentrations peaked after four capsules 10 mg in the first hour at 97.5 μg/l (median) within a wide range and then declined rapidly to 57.3 μg/l (median) at 5 h. The mean concentrations did not vary along the following 4 days and were similar in both groups. After 120 h, medians of 25.5 μg/l in the slow-release group and 14.6 μg/l in the GITS group, respectively, were achieved.
The strength of our study is the study design (inclusion, exclusion criteria; time schedule of intervention; technical handling of the blood sample) realized in pregnant women with preterm labor. The results are therefore really representative for patients with nifedipine tocolysis and the objective of the study has been attained although it could be argued against the sample size of n = 14. However, it is an evident problem of pharmacokinetic studies that sample size is limited in patients rather than in healthy volunteers because of the clinical feasibility.
The pharmacokinetics after nifedipine capsules in pregnancy have been studied by Ferguson et al. in 12 patients with preterm labor [8]. They found a similar range after 4 capsules taken within 60 min (mean 96.7 ± S.D. 45.3; range 23.4–197.7 μg/l) as we in our study (19.9–292.0 μg/l). In patients with cardiovascular diseases the therapeutic nifedipine blood level is 10–100 μg/l (29–289 nmol/l; 1 μg/l = 2.9 nmol/l) [4]. In our study the nifedipine level exceeded 100 μg/l in six patients within the first hour and in one patient of group 1 (slow-release tablets) at 5 h and at 96 h. No group 2 patient (GITS) had nifedipine concentrations >100 μg/l after the first hour.
Previous studies with nifedipine slow-release or GITS tablets in non-pregnant healthy volunteers have also shown a great interindividual variability of nifedipine plasma concentrations [5], [9], [10]. However, concentrations measured in our pregnant women (at 120 h: 25.0 μg/l after slow-release tablets and 14.6 μg/l after GITS tablets; median) were clearly lower than that in non-pregnant women: Toal et al. found a mean Cmax of 85.5 mg/l after slow-release tablets 20 mg in a total dose of 40 mg/d and 30.5 μg/l after a single tablet GITS 60 mg [5]; a similar value was also achieved in the study of Tateishi et al. (Cmax = 43.5 μg/l after a single tablet GITS 60 mg) [9]; in the study of Crome et al. mean plasma concentrations after one tablet GITS 60 mg/d for 7 days varied between 32 and 60 μg/l and were higher in younger than in elderly volunteers [10]. Direct comparison of our to these results are difficult because doses and regimen were different. However, physiologic changes of pregnancy may affect distribution and excretion when even higher doses in our study resulted in lower levels than in non-pregnant volunteers. Factors determining the results such as technical handling or concomitant food are not very probable. Technical handling of the blood sample was performed under strong exclusion of light exposure. Nifedipine tablets were administered before breakfast or at least 2 h after a meal. Other factors may also play an important role such as the influence of pharmacogenetic differences. Oxidation polymorphism for nifedipine was detected in 1984, and healthy non-pregnant women and men were differentiated into slow (17% of volunteers) and rapid metabolisers (83%) [11]. Also in Koreans a lower clearance for nifedipine has been found than in Caucasians [12]. In fact, we did not see any difference between our three women from Asia and the Caucasians; further studies with a larger number of volunteers should clarify this aspect. Tsatsaris et al. have shown in a meta-analysis of nine studies (n = 679), that neonatal mortality after tocolysis with nifedipine versus betaagonists was not increased and neonatal morbidity was slightly better after nifedipine. The rate of admissions to the neonatal intensive care unit was 33% whereas in the present study it was similar or even lower (21%).
In summary, we have demonstrated plasma concentrations after a loading dose of nifedipine 10 mg capsules, 40 mg over 1 h followed by a maintenance dose with slow-release or GITS tablets. GITS tablets 90 mg/d are an alternative dosage regimen to slow-release tablets 60 mg/d for tocolysis with similar pharmacokinetic profile and a good tolerance. In patients with GITS we observed a smaller inter-individual variation of kinetic profile than in the slow-release group. Moreover, tocolysis with GITS tablets is simpler than that with slow-release tablets and may be associated with a higher compliance. GITS tablets are therefore also qualified for outpatients. For future management of nifedipine tocolysis it has also to be evaluated if a loading dose of 20 or 30 mg instead of 40 mg would be sufficient and even related with less adverse effects such as headache.

    Obstetric pharmacokinetic dosing studies are urgently needed
    McCormack S.A., Best B.M.
    Frontiers in Pediatrics • Volume 2 • 2014 • Article 9
    Use of pharmacotherapy during pregnancy is common and increasing. Physiologic changes during pregnancy may significantly alter the overall systemic drug exposure, necessitating dose changes. A search of PubMed for pharmacokinetic clinical trials showed 494 publications during pregnancy out of 35,921 total pharmacokinetic published studies (1.29%), from the late 1960s through August 31, 2013. Closer examination of pharmacokinetic studies in pregnant women published since 2008 (81 studies) revealed that about a third of the trials were for treatment of acute labor and delivery issues, a third included studies of infectious disease treatment during pregnancy, and the remaining third were for varied ante-partum indications. Approximately, two-thirds of these recent studies were primarily funded by government agencies worldwide, one-quarter were supported by private non-profit foundations or combinations of government and private funding, and slightly <10% were supported by pharmaceutical industry. As highlighted in this review, vast gaps exist in pharmacology information and evidence for appropriate dosing of medications in pregnant women. This lack of knowledge and understanding of drug disposition throughout pregnancy place both the mother and the fetus at risk for avoidable therapeutic misadventures – suboptimal efficacy or excess toxicity – with medication use in pregnancy. Increased efforts to perform and support obstetric dosing and pharmacokinetic studies are greatly needed.
