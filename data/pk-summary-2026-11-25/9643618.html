    Distribution and excretion of venlafaxine and O-desmethylvenlafaxine in human milk

    Abstract

    Aims

    To characterise the transfer of venlafaxine (V) and its O-desmethyl metabolite (ODV) into human milk by measuring milk/plasma (M/P) ratio, and to estimate the likely dose received by a breast-fed infant.

    Methods

    Milk and plasma samples were collected from three lactating women who were taking venlafaxine for depression, and were at steady-state. In two of the patients, venous blood and milk samples were collected 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 12 h post dose, while in the third patient a single pair of blood and milk samples was obtained 0.83 h post dose. A plasma sample was obtained from each of their infants. V and ODV were measured in plasma and milk by high performance liquid chromatography. M/P was calculated and infant dose estimated as drug concentration in milk×a milk intake of 0.15 l kg−1 day−1, relative to the weight-adjusted maternal dose.

    Results

    Mean M/P for V was 4.1 (range 2.8–4.8) and 3.1 for ODV (range 2.8–3.8). The mean total infant dose (as V equivalents) was 7.6% (range 4.7–9.2%) of the maternal weight-adjusted dose, with approximately equal amounts of V (3.5%) and ODV (4.1%) in the dose. ODV (median 100 μg l−1) was detected in the plasma of all three infants. The infants were healthy and showed no acute adverse effects.

    Conclusions

    These preliminary data show that the total dose of V and ODV ingested by breast-fed infants can be as high as 9.2% of maternal intake. Moreover there were measurable concentrations of ODV in the infants’ plasma. We recommend that exposed infants should be observed closely.

    Keywords: human milk, venlafaxine, milk:plasma ratio, infant dose

    Introduction

    Venlafaxine (V) is a new phenethylamine bicyclic antidepressant that inhibits the reuptake of both serotonin and noradrenaline [1, 2]. Because of its unique mode of action, it is likely to be prescribed increasingly where the tricyclic, tetracyclic and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressants (SSRI) have failed [1, 3]. Inevitably, because of the 10–15% incidence of depression in the puerperal period [4] together with an approximate 3–12% incidence of depression in the general population [5], such use will include pregnant and/or lactating women. Breast-feeding is widely promoted because it offers a variety of benefits to both the infant and mother [6]. Hence, knowledge of the extent to which new antidepressants such as V are distributed into, and excreted from human milk is necessary so that infant dose and the potential for adverse effects can be assessed.

    V has an oral bioavailability of around 92%, a time of peak in plasma of 2–4 h and a steady-state volume of distribution of 6–7 l kg−1 [1, 2, 7]. Plasma protein binding is low at about 27% [1]. It undergoes first pass metabolism to O-desmethylvenlafaxine (ODV) which is the major metabolite in man and also has antidepressant activity [8]. Other reported metabolites include N-desmethylvenlafaxine, N,O-di-desmethylvenlafaxine as well as other conjugated metabolites [9]. In vitro studies indicate that cytochrome P4502D6 (CYP2D6) is responsible for the demethylation of V and that the drug also is a weak inhibitor of CYP2D6 mediated metabolism of other drug substrates [10]. Mean elimination half-lives for V and ODV are around 5 h and 11 h respectively [8]. Steady-state plasma concentrations of ODV range from 1–2 times those for V [8]. Both hepatic and renal dysfunction can significantly decrease the clearance of V and ODV (Efexor® package insert, [11]).

    In the present study we have documented the milk/plasma (M/P) ratio for V and ODV in human milk and estimated the likely dose exposure for the breast-fed infant.

    Methods

    Patients and study design

    The study design was approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of the King Edward Memorial Hospital, Centre for Women's Health. Written informed consent was obtained from three patients who were taking V twice daily for a major depressive disorder (1 and 2) or for post-natal depression (3). Two patients (1 and 2) were admitted to the research ward at 07.30 h, and an intravenous cannula was inserted into a forearm vein prior to the morning dose at 08.00 h. Venous blood samples (8 ml) were collected from the cannula into heparinised tubes at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 h post dose, and by venepuncture at 12 h. The patients were discharged from hospital after the 8 h samples and milk and plasma samples at 12 h post dose were collected by a research assistant who visited the patient at home. At the same time intervals samples of milk (15 ml) were obtained (approximately equal amounts from each breast) via an electric or manual breast pump. In the third patient (2), a single pair of milk (15 ml) and heparinised blood samples were obtained 0.83 h after the morning dose of V. Milk and plasma were retained for subsequent drug assay. The pH of the milk was measured only for patient 2. The infants were allowed to breast-feed according to their normal pattern during the study. A venous blood sample (0.5–1 ml) was taken from all three infants and plasma (heparinised) was retained for subsequent drug assay.

    High performance liquid chromatography

    Following the addition of norclozapine (200 ng) as an internal standard, 1 ml aliquots of plasma were made alkaline with 0.5 ml 2% w/v sodium tetraborate (pH 9.2) and the analytes were extracted into 10 ml diethylether by shaking vigorously for 5 min. After centrifugation (2000 g for 5 min), the ether phase (9 ml) was back-extracted into 0.2 ml 0.1 m H2SO4 by vortexing for 1 min, and 0.05–0.1 ml aliquots of the acid phase were injected onto the h.p.l.c. Milk samples were extracted in a similar manner except that the method of addition was used to enable correction for differing recoveries that may arise because of varying lipid content of the samples. V and ODV concentrations in milk are determined by taking four equal aliquots of each milk sample (0.1–1 ml), adjusting the volume to 1 ml as necessary, and spiking three of these aliquots with increasing concentrations of authentic V and ODV. The samples were then extracted and analysed as for plasma. The h.p.l.c. system consisted of an RP Select B column (E. Merck Co), and a solvent of 20% v/v acetonitrile in 0.01% w/v NaCl and 0.02% v/v H3PO4, pumped at 1.4 ml min−1. Eluting compounds were detected by their u.v. absorbance at 229 nm. Plasma V and ODV concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve (peak height ratio analyte:norclozapine vs analyte concentration; r2>0.995) run with each batch of samples. For each individual milk sample, a standard curve (peak height ratio analyte:norclozapine vs added analyte concentration; r2>0.995) was constructed and analyte and drug concentrations were determined from the negative x-axis intercept, with appropriate correction for dilution. The coefficients of variation (CV; n=5) for the plasma assay were 2.2 and 2.0% at 25 and 1500 μg l −1 respectively for V and 4.4 and 3.3% at 50 and 1500 μg l −1 respectively for ODV. For the milk assay CVs were 6.5 and 4.7% at 500 and 5000 μg l −1 respectively for V and 3.4 and 2.9% at 1500 and 3000 μg l −1 respectively for ODV. In both milk and plasma, the limit of quantitation (CV <20%) was 10 μg l−1 for V and ODV and the limit of detection (signal:noise=4) was 4 μg l−1for V and 3 μg l−1for ODV.

    Calculation of M/P ratio and infant dose

    M/P for patients 1 and 2 was calculated from the area under the milk and plasma concentration-time curves (AUC; estimated by the trapezoidal rule), and from a single pair of milk and plasma concentration measurements in patient 3. The absolute infant dose of V and ODV was calculated from the average drug concentration over the 12 h dose interval (patients 1 and 2) or from a single milk concentration measurement (patient 3), assuming an infant milk consumption of 0.15 l kg−1 d−1 [12] and an oral bioavailability of 100%. The relative doses of V and ODV (in V equivalents) received by a nursing infant, were derived from the calculated infant intake ×100, divided by the maternal dose where both doses were expressed in mg kg−1 day−1.

    Data analysis

    Data have been summarised as mean±s.e.mean (or range), or median and inter-quartile range as appropriate.

    Results

    The mothers in the study were aged 26, 35, and 30 years and weighed 73, 55 and 49 kg respectively. They had been taking a stable dose of V for 0.5, 5 and 0.23 months at the time of study and were therefore at steady-state. Their infants were aged 1.3, 6 and 0.37 months (38, 38 and 40 weeks at delivery respectively) and weighed 4.0, 7.2 and 3.5 kg respectively at the time of study. The milk and plasma concentration-time profiles for patients 1 and 2 are shown in Figure 1. M/P for V (range 2.8–4.8) and ODV (range 2.8–3.8) at individual time points varied considerably with the largest values at the time of peak plasma concentration (1–3 h). In patient 3, where only a single pair of milk and plasma samples was available, M/P for V and ODV were 5.18 and 3.07 respectively. The maternal doses of V, M/P ratios (estimated from AUC data for patients 1 and 2 and from a single time point for patient 3) and estimated infant doses of V and ODV are summarised in the Table 1. The mean total infant dose (as V equivalents) was 7.6% (range 4.7–9.2%) of the maternal weight-adjusted dose. V and ODV contributed approximately equally to the dose. In addition, the pharmacologically active metabolite ODV was detected in the plasma of all three infants (Table 1). As assessed by the attending physician, and by questioning the mothers on the study day, the infants were healthy, showed no acute adverse effects, and were within the population 95 percentile range for weight.

    Discussion

    The present study addresses the need for detailed studies of SSRIs and related antidepressants in lactation [6, 13]. Both V and ODV partitioned well into milk as indicated by the M/P values. The variability in M/P for both drugs at individual time points illustrates the importance of using the AUC method for calculating M/P so that time-related variations can be minimised. The mechanism of the increased drug partitioning at the time of the peak concentrations is not known. Milk pH was measured only in patient 2, and remained relatively constant over the study (7.2±0.1). Thus for this patient, time-dependent alterations in pH do not assist in explaining this increased partition. The time-dependency of M/P may explain the somewhat higher value of 5.18 for V for patient 3 where a single observation was made at 0.83 h after dose. However, at the time of study, this patient had only been breast feeding for approximately 1 week and immature milk composition could also be a cause of variability in M/P.

    The maternal plasma and milk drug concentrations were sufficiently high that calculated dose of V and ODV approached the notional 10%‘level of concern’ for intake of drugs in milk [14]. However, despite a low assay detection limit for both analytes, only ODV was detected in the infant's plasma. Since the infants received both V and ODV in their milk, this indicates that they have significant capacity to demethylate the parent drug, even at the age of 10 days (infant of patient 3). It also suggests that they had the CYP2D6 extensive metabolizer phenotype.

    Our study did not find any short-term adverse effects in any of the three breast-feed infants. However, since the infant dose was high compared with published data for other antidepressants [6], there were measurable concentrations of ODV in the infant's plasma, and our data set is small, we recommend that exposed infants should be monitored closely for short-term adverse effects such as agitation, insomnia, poor feeding or failure to thrive. The long-term effects of antidepressant exposure on cognitive development of infants are largely unknown [13]. However, there are individual studies showing that the tricyclic antidepressant dothiepin [15] and the SSRI fluoxetine [16] have no adverse effects at 3 and up to 7 years respectively.
