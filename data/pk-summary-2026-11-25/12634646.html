Abstract

Objective: The purpose of this study was to characterize the pharmacokinetics of orally administered azithromycin in the term gravid woman. Study Design: Twenty women who were scheduled for elective cesarean delivery were enrolled prospectively and received 1 g of oral azithromycin at either 6, 12, 24, 72, or 168 hours before the operation. All women received spinal anesthesia, at which time a sample of cerebrospinal fluid was obtained for analysis. Maternal serum and urine were obtained immediately before the operation. Intraoperatively, samples of myometrium, maternal adipose tissue, placenta, amniotic fluid, and umbilical arterial and venous cord blood were obtained. Azithromycin levels were determined quantitatively with high-pressure liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection. Results: All participants tolerated the preoperative azithromycin without significant adverse reactions. Peak maternal serum azithromycin levels occurred within 6 hours of drug administration. Although high serum levels of azithromycin were reached early, a rapid decline in drug concentration was noted over the initial 24 hours after the drug administration (6-hour: 311 ng/mL; 24-hour: 63 ng/mL). In contrast, azithromycin levels in myometrial, adipose, and placental tissue were higher (>500 ng/mL) and sustained for up to 72 hours after administration. High urine levels of azithromycin (>5000 ng/mL) were noted similarly during the initial 72 hours after drug administration. Umbilical arterial and venous serum azithromycin levels were low (19-38 ng/mL) during the first 72 hours. Amniotic fluid levels were highest at 6 hours (151 ng/mL) and declined rapidly. Maternal cerebrospinal azithromycin concentrations were undetectable for all time points. Conclusion: Azithromycin has a rapid serum half-life in the term gravid woman with a prolonged half-life and high-sustained antibiotic levels noted within myometrium, adipose, and placental tissue. Given the broad antimicrobial spectrum and placental penetration, azithromycin may have potential use for the treatment of perinatal infections. (Am J Obstet Gynecol 2003;188:714-8.)

Azithromycin is an effective macrolide antibiotic that is structurally similar to erythromycin.1, 2 In contrast to erythromycin, however, azithromycin has a prolonged duration of action, improved tissue penetration, and an extended range of antimicrobial coverage.1, 2 Azithromycin has a long intracellular half-life in adults (exceeding 60 hours in tissue) and a large volume of distribution (23 L/kg).1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Peak adult serum concentrations are attained within 12 to 24 hours after oral dosing, with rapid uptake into lymphatics and tissues with tissue concentrations that exceed serum concentrations by 10- to 100-fold.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Azithromycin is taken up preferentially by leukocytes and macrophages with tissue concentrations that exceed serum concentrations by 10- to 100-fold.6, 7 In addition to the extended tissue penetration and prolonged systemic intracellular half-life, azithromycin has an improved side effect profile compared with erythromycin.6 Fewer adverse effects overall are reported with azithromycin compared with erythromycin, including gastrointestinal effects, headache, skin rash, and dizziness.6 Approximately 20% of patients discontinue erythromycin because of side effects, compared with only 1% of azithromycin users.6
Similar to the other macrolide antibiotics, the principal mechanism of action of azithromycin is through binding with the 50S ribosomal subunit and inhibition of messenger RNA-directed polypeptide synthesis. It is bacteriostatic or bacteriocidal, depending on the organism. The antimicrobial spectrum of azithromycin is broad and provides adequate coverage for a variety of microorganisms: Staphylococcus species, Streptococcus species, and Haemophilus influenzae and a number of common gynecologic infectious agents including Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Ureaplasma urealyticum. 2, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Recent data have demonstrated better tolerance and equal effectiveness of azithromycin versus erythromycin in the treatment of chlamydial cervicitis during pregnancy.12 Azithromycin is widely used for the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia and chlamydia in pregnancy. Azithromycin has been categorized as a pregnancy category B drug by the Food and Drug Administration.13 Animal studies in which up to 60 times the human-equivalent dose has been administered have shown no decreased fertility or harmful effects on animal fetuses.2, 13 To date, there are no data that document adverse effects on human fetuses.13
The broad range of antimicrobial coverage, the deep tissue penetration, and the prolonged duration of action make azithromycin an interesting prospect for the treatment and prevention of a variety of perinatal bacterial infections. Azithromycin's antimicrobial spectrum includes the parasite Toxoplasma gondii and the spirochete Treponema pallidum. 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 In spite of the great potential for the use for azithromycin in pregnancy, limited data are available regarding the maternal and transplacental pharmacokinetics of azithromycin in the gravid patient.23, 24To address this important issue, we conducted a prospective study in term gravid women to characterize the pharmacokinetics and transplacental penetration of azithromycin.

Material and methods

The investigation was conducted after Institutional Review Board approval from Mayo Medical Center and the University of Alabama at Birmingham. Women with a term (≥37 weeks of gestation) singleton intrauterine pregnancy who were scheduled for an elective primary or repeat cesarean delivery were eligible for the investigation. Women with a recent history of pregnancy-induced hypertension, preeclampsia, diabetes mellitus, intrauterine growth restriction, or abruptio placentae were excluded from participation because of potential compromise of the maternal-placental-fetal unit.
Women who consented to participation were assigned to receive a standard 1-g oral suspension dose packet of azithromycin (Zithromax; Pfizer Pharmaceutical Co, New York, NY) orally as preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis at either a 6-, 12-, 24-, 72-, or 168-hour interval before scheduled cesarean delivery. The time group assignment was made on the basis of the anticipated scheduling of the surgical procedure. All participants were instructed to take the medication either 1 hour before or 2 hours after eating and to avoid the use of aluminum- or magnesium-containing antacids before the administration of the medication because these factors can impair drug absorption. After the elapse of the specified time interval, the patient underwent scheduled cesarean delivery. Before the initiation of the operative procedure and before the initiation of perioperative hydration, samples of maternal blood and urine were collected. All women received spinal anesthesia, at which time a sample of cerebrospinal fluid was obtained for analysis. Intraoperatively, samples of amniotic fluid, umbilical arterial and venous cord blood, placental tissue, maternal myometrium, and maternal adipose tissue were obtained. All liquid specimens were centrifuged for 5 minutes at 5000 revolutions/min, after which the supernatant was collected and frozen at −70°C until analysis. Solid tissue specimens were frozen and stored at −70°C until analysis. Maternal and neonatal outcomes were followed on all study participants through the standard 6-week postoperative evaluation.
Quantitative determinations of azithromycin levels in collected specimens were measured with proprietary high-performance liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection (BAS Analytics, West Lafayette, Ind). The assay limit of detection for azithromycin was 10.4 ng/mL. Intra-assay and interassay variability was <10% for the azithromycin assay that was used. Solid tissue specimens were homogenized before being assayed for drug levels. Pharmacokinetic assay services were provided by BAS Analytics and the Pfizer Research Laboratory. Pooled data at each time point were averaged and expressed as the mean with respective SD. Estimated half-life values of azithromycin in each of the specimen groups were derived by fitting the data with a nonlinear least squares regression method to a single exponential (first order) decay model with Sigma Plot 5.1 (SPSS Science Inc, Chicago, Ill).

The estimated half-life of azithromycin was approximately 70 hours in myometrium, >70 hours in maternal adipose tissue, 11 hours in urine, and 12 hours in serum. For the fetal specimens, the estimated half-life of azithromycin was approximately 70 hours in placental tissue, 30 hours in amniotic fluid, and 12 hours in umbilical cord serum.
No significant adverse outcomes were noted in the women and infants who were delivered as part of this study. One maternal complication, wound cellulitis at approximately 5 weeks after delivery, was noted. Three infants had minor complications: Oral thrush at 2 weeks(n = 1), dermatitis at 2 weeks (n = 1), and a febrile workup at 1 months (n = 1).

Comment

Increasingly, azithromycin is being used in pregnancy for a variety of indications that include the treatment of chlamydial cervicitis and community-acquired pneumonia. Recently, great interest has emerged regarding the potential use of azithromycin for the treatment and prevention of a variety of perinatal infections. Azithromycin's antimicrobial spectrum includes the parasite, T gondii, and the spirochete, T pallidum. 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 These organisms can cause perinatal infection (congenital toxoplasmosis and syphilis, respectively) of the fetus, with potential catastrophic pregnancy complications. The extensive deep tissue penetration and a prolonged duration of action of azithromycin make it an appealing alternative to treatment options that are currently available during pregnancy. The lack of sufficient data on the pharmacokinetics and transplacental passage of azithromycin in pregnancy has limited research on this potentially important use of azithromycin.
To date, few studies have attempted to characterize the transplacental pharmacokinetics of azithromycin. Heikkinen et al23 provided initial insights with regard to this important issue in their study of the transplacental transfer of the macrolide antibiotics, that included azithromycin, with an in vitro placental cotyledon perfusion model. These investigators demonstrated limited in vitro transplacental transfer of azithromycin, similar to that observed with erythromycin. In contrast to these observations, Stray-Petersen et al24 conducted a small observational study of pregnant women who received azithromycin for the treatment of toxoplasmosis and noted that azithromycin passed into the fetal compartment with high levels within the placental tissue.
In this investigation, we have characterized the maternal and transplacental pharmacokinetics of azithromycin in the pregnancy. Our data demonstrate penetration of azithromycin into the fetal compartment with high placental levels that were reached and maintained for up to 72 hours. Amniotic fluid concentration of azithromycin was noted, with a gradual decline through 72 hours. Fetal serum levels of azithromycin, however, were low (approximately 20%-50% less than maternal serum levels) and appeared to peak by 12 to 24 hours. The sustained levels of azithromycin that were achieved in the maternal myometrium, adipose tissue, urine, and placental tissue were well above the reported minimal inhibitory concentration for C trachomatis (30-125 ng/mL), N gonorrhoeae (16-64 ng/mL), Staphylococcus spp (64-500 ng/mL), Streptococcus spp (64-500 ng/mL), and a number of other microorganisms.8 Nonsustained levels of azithromycin were attained in maternal serum and amniotic fluid. Both maternal cerebrospinal fluid levels and umbilical cord serum levels of azithromycin were below the reported minimal inhibitory concentration for these organisms. Although our investigation is limited by the cross-sectional nature, the relatively small sample size for each time point evaluated, and the single-dose regimen used, the study provides important insights into the pharmacokinetics and transplacental passage of azithromycin in the term gravid woman. Although these findings are important, they may not be generalizable to earlier gestational ages, when the potential use of azithromycin might be of use clinically for the treatment of perinatal infection. The low fetal serum and amniotic fluid levels of azithromycin that were noted in this investigation raise concerns regarding the actual fetal levels of azithromycin that are achieved. Thus, in the absence of additional pharmacokinetic data that are related specifically to fetal tissue levels, the use of azithromycin for the treatment of perinatal fetal infections, such as syphilis or toxoplasmosis, cannot be recommended. Clearly, further research is needed to explore the in vivo transplacental and fetal pharmacokinetics of both single- and repeat-dose azithromycin regimens. Such data will provide the basis for investigations that are designed to evaluate the efficacy of azithromycin for the treatment and/or prevention of perinatal infections.

    Azithromycin: Mechanisms of action and their relevance for clinical applications
    Parnham M.J., …, Vos R.
    Pharmacology and Therapeutics • Volume 143 • 2014
    Azithromycin is a macrolide antibiotic which inhibits bacterial protein synthesis, quorum-sensing and reduces the formation of biofilm. Accumulating effectively in cells, particularly phagocytes, it is delivered in high concentrations to sites of infection, as reflected in rapid plasma clearance and extensive tissue distribution. Azithromycin is indicated for respiratory, urogenital, dermal and other bacterial infections, and exerts immunomodulatory effects in chronic inflammatory disorders, including diffuse panbronchiolitis, post-transplant bronchiolitis and rosacea. Modulation of host responses facilitates its long-term therapeutic benefit in cystic fibrosis, non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and non-eosinophilic asthma.
    Initial, stimulatory effects of azithromycin on immune and epithelial cells, involving interactions with phospholipids and Erk1/2, are followed by later modulation of transcription factors AP-1, NFκB, inflammatory cytokine and mucin release. Delayed inhibitory effects on cell function and high lysosomal accumulation accompany disruption of protein and intracellular lipid transport, regulation of surface receptor expression, of macrophage phenotype and autophagy. These later changes underlie many immunomodulatory effects of azithromycin, contributing to resolution of acute infections and reduction of exacerbations in chronic airway diseases. A sub-group of post-transplant bronchiolitis patients appears to be sensitive to azithromycin, as may be patients with severe sepsis. Other promising indications include chronic prostatitis and periodontitis, but weak activity in malaria is unlikely to prove crucial. Long-term administration of azithromycin must be balanced against the potential for increased bacterial resistance. Azithromycin has a very good record of safety, but recent reports indicate rare cases of cardiac torsades des pointes in patients at risk.
